# The Creative Act (Rick Rubin)

Operating rules distilled from Rubin's book. Every project moves through four phases: **Seeds → Experimentation → Craft → Completion**. The phases are not a line: work slides back to an earlier phase whenever something new shows up. Most creative trouble is a phase error: judging in a gathering phase, gathering in a finishing phase.

## Steps

1. **Locate the phase.** Ask what exists now: loose fragments (Seeds), a few fragments being played with (Experimentation), a direction with a clear form (Craft), or a near-final piece (Completion). Name the phase to the user. Done when you and the user agree on one phase.
2. **Diagnose against the phase table** below. Match the user's symptom to a row; the cure comes from that row, not from a later phase.
3. **Prescribe one concrete move**, not a philosophy lecture. Prefer an experiment the user can run today over advice. When the user is stuck, reach for an exercise in [`references/exercises.md`](references/exercises.md).
4. **Keep the user's taste as the judge.** You offer options, comparisons and cuts; the user decides by what pulls them forward. Your preference is one more data point, never the verdict.

## Phase table

| Phase           | Job                                 | Leading rule                        | Typical failure                                           | Cure                                                                        |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Seeds           | Collect starting points             | Gather, never rank                  | Discarding small ideas; waiting for one big sign          | Collect for weeks; the size of a seed says nothing about its yield          |
| Experimentation | Find how a seed wants to grow       | Generate, never prune               | Steering a seed to a preplanned result; editing too early | Try every direction; follow excitement; shelve (don't bin) seeds that stall |
| Craft           | Build the form that revealed itself | Add, then prune to feed the core    | Endless polishing; demo-itis; stuck on one section        | Private deadlines; skip the hard part and finish around it; step away       |
| Completion      | Release it                          | The work is done when it feels done | Fake flaws; fear of permanence; waiting for perfect       | A real due date; the ruthless edit; start new seeds to pull you out         |

## Core rules

- **Excitement is the compass.** The body's pull, the urge to lean forward, is the signal Rubin calls the ecstatic. It picks which seed to grow, which experiment to craft, which of two options wins. It comes before analysis; analysis explains afterwards. When the energy in a piece is gone for a long time, back up to where the charge was or pick a new seed.
- **Test, don't debate.** An idea that seems weak in the head can work in the world and the reverse. Build, play or mock each option; never choose between descriptions. The person with the idea demonstrates it. Persuasion drives toward the average.
- **Choose by A/B.** Compare two options side by side, blind when possible. If neither pulls, flip a coin and notice which side you hope for. Go with the first reaction, not the reasoned second one.
- **Rules are assumptions.** Genre, format and your own proven method are defaults, not laws. Try the opposite of any rule, or push it to the extreme. The hidden rules you follow unaware are the strongest; surface them. Beginner's mind: act as if you never learned the conventions.
- **Temporary rules create character.** A deliberate constraint per project (one colour, one instrument, no letter "e", a new tool) forces non-obvious solutions. Keep it strictly or drop it on purpose.
- **Lower the stakes.** Treat this piece as one small work in a long series, an experiment whose result feeds the next one. Doubt the work, which can improve it; never doubt yourself, which only freezes you.
- **Essence over ornament.** Every work has a core that makes it itself. Remove parts until it stops being the same work; if you question whether an element serves the piece, cut it. Anything cut can come back.
- **Context changes content.** When a piece falls flat, change what surrounds it: neighbour, order, frame, scale, contrast pairs (loud–soft, fast–slow, near–far).
- **Abundance.** Use your best idea now; more come when the channel flows. Hoarding and perfectionism stop the flow. Each finished work is one diary entry, not the whole life.
- **Audience last.** Make the work you love; commercial prediction is out of your control and rarely works. Success is the private moment you release a work you are proud of.

## Craft-phase traps

- **Demo-itis**: repeated exposure to a rough draft makes it feel final, so later improvements feel wrong. Cure: don't replay, reread or show a draft unless you are actively working on it.
- **Clean slate**: after long work you lose perspective. Step fully away, then come back as a first-time viewer. When checking revisions, never tick off the old change list; make a new list from scratch on a fresh pass, because each change can break something else.
- **Vision beyond skill**: the imagined version is out of reach. Build the practical version; it may be better. Skill is learned, so "not yet", not "can't".
- **Stuck section**: jump over it, finish everything else, return when the context on both sides defines the bridge.
- **All-or-nothing**: one flaw makes you want to bin the whole piece. Look for the 80% that works; fix or remove the weak 20%.

## Completion

- Share the near-final work with one person to _hear it anew yourself_, not to collect votes.
- Feedback tells you about the giver as much as the work. Treat a proposed fix as a pointer to a real problem ("cut the bridge" → "look again at the bridge").
- **Ruthless edit**: cut far below the final size (an album of twenty tracks down to the five you can't live without, a long draft down to a third), then add back only what makes it better, not more.
- Art divides. If everyone likes it, it may not go far enough.
- Plant new seeds now; the pull of the next project helps close this one.
